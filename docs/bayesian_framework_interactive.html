How confident are we in a link's category?

Start here — what this is, what it assumes, and how to use it

What this is: an interactive guide to the Bayesian framework developed in the Supplementary Information of Contextual evidence for classifying interactions and guiding their discovery. The taxonomy assigns each candidate link to one of eight categories from three pieces of evidence — the model prediction Y, the local observation Ol, and the replicate observation Or. That assignment is deterministic, but every source is error-prone, so it carries uncertainty. The framework therefore treats the true category C as unknown and returns a posterior P(C | E) over the eight.

Scope: the upper panels follow the simplest version of the framework — binary evidence, one symmetric error rate per source, a uniform prior, and independent errors. The two lower panels follow the grounded and cumulative version, where the local axis errs at different rates in each direction and the replicate count is retained. Throughout, the illustrative rates are εY = 0.2, εl = 0.3, εr = 0.1, ρ = 0.15 and f = 0.05, chosen to show the behaviour of the framework rather than to describe any particular system.

How to use: pick a category on the taxonomy tree. Its error-free signature z_c = (1, 0, 1) becomes the observed evidence E, and the confidence in that label is (1−εY)(1−εl)(1−εr).

Try this: pick possibly missing — predicted and seen elsewhere, but not here. Open Error rates and push εl up: confidence in the label falls and recurrent takes the difference, because the more local sampling misses, the likelier the link was simply overlooked. Then switch to Extension and watch confidence climb as replicates accumulate — and stop at κ.

Category prior (base rates) — uniform by default; edit to break the symmetry

A uniform prior treats all eight categories as equally probable before any evidence arrives, so confidence in the chosen label depends only on the error rates — not on which category it is. Making some categories more probable than others would make the posterior no longer equal to the likelihood. Weights are relative, rescaled to sum to 1 (shown as %).

The posterior over all eight categories under the scenario you picked, so the eight lines sum to 1 at every R. The eight fall into four pairs, one per cell of the confusion matrix: the two members of a pair share Y and Ol and differ only on Or, so colour marks the pair and line style the replicate bit — solid observed elsewhere, dashed not. The chosen category and its replicate counterpart are one such pair, drawn bold with markers. The full count n is retained throughout, which is what lets confidence accumulate: had the count been collapsed to the single bit Or = 1[n ≥ 1], the same replicates would push confidence the other way, towards half of κ. Switch the scenario to see the mirror image. Note that the local axis is directional here: a link that is there is missed at εl, and one that is not there is recorded at f. That is what makes κ larger than the (1−εY)(1−εl) of the simple version.

Effort to confidence — how many replicates? — the same curves read as a design question

Instead of asking "how confident am I after R replicates?", we can ask: "how many replicates of consistent evidence do I need to reach a confidence target?" That count is R*. The two lines are the chosen category and its replicate counterpart — the two halves of one confusion cell (same Y and Ol, opposite Or). One is corroborated by detections (it keeps being seen elsewhere), the other by absences (it keeps not being seen). Pick any category on the tree to re-pair; ρ and f are shared with the panel above, and the count n is retained throughout.

The two lines are one pair, so they share a colour with the plot above and differ only in style: solid is the category observed elsewhere, dashed the one that is not. Both rise with consistent replicates toward the shared maximum contextual confidence κ — here the y axis is scaled so that κ is the top of the plot, so a line reaching the top edge has extracted everything replicates can give. The y values are real confidences, and so is the target: the orange line sits at the absolute confidence you asked for, and each R* dot marks where a curve crosses it. The target must lie strictly below κ, since replicates buy only speed towards the maximum that εY, εl and f impose; raising it requires a better model or a complementary local method. A category and its counterpart differ only in the replicate term, so their log-odds grow linearly in R, but at very different rates: a detection is rare under either hypothesis and so is surprising, whereas an empty replicate is what both mostly predict. The detection-corroborated line is therefore steeper and the absence-corroborated one lags. R* depends on ρ only through its contrast with f: if f = 0 the factor p1R cancels and confidence sits at κ from the first replicate, while as f approaches p1 R* climbs and then becomes infinite, a detection then being evidence for a false detection. Replicates are worth taking only while they are cleaner than the link is rare.
